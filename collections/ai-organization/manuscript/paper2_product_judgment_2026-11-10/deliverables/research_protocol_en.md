# Building product judgment: research and training companion

Version: 10 November 2026. This companion specifies proposed research. No participant experiment or effect estimation has been conducted.

## Research question and scope

The question is how evidence and diagnosis in project A can improve decisions in project B when AI reduces some exploration costs. Units of analysis include teams, projects and decision episodes. Improving an artefact within one project does not establish learning across projects.

Scientific entrepreneurship, customer discovery and conversational learning with AI already provide relevant foundations. The proposed contribution concerns retaining conditions, comparing them and predicting outcomes in new projects, including circumstances that produce negative transfer. This is a research claim whose distinctiveness still requires a fuller comparison with the closest literature.

Customer value, investment quality and transferable judgment are separate outcomes. A commercially unsuccessful project may leave useful knowledge, while short-term success may coexist with a mistaken explanation.

## A preliminary study using public records

Begin by reconstructing decisions and classifying evidence, without treating launch counts as a learning measure. Seek successive projects by the same team and include continuation, pivots, termination and unknown status. Potential materials include dated product updates, pricing announcements, founder accounts, customer feedback and closure notices. Access conditions, archival completeness and identity matching require individual checks.

| Field | Coding rule | Invalid substitution |
|---|---|---|
| Team–project link | Check public attribution and cross-links; retain uncertainty | Identical aliases imply identical teams |
| Decision time | Separate event, publication and collection dates | Publication date equals decision date |
| Prior judgment | Require evidence that the statement preceded the outcome | Reconstruct predictions from hindsight |
| Customer context | Record task, frequency, alternatives, user and payer | Industry label equals customer definition |
| Evidence type | Separate interest, task behaviour, reuse and payment | Votes, registration and payment are equivalent |
| Decision | Continue, pivot, stop or unresolved, with supporting source | Inactive website implies deliberate termination |
| AI use | Separate self-report, documented process and unknown | Fast release implies AI adoption |
| Later project | Identify condition checking and new predictions | A changed product name proves a new project |

Test whether records cover prior judgments, subsequent evidence and the next project before expanding the sample. Independent coding, disagreement resolution and reliability assessment must actually be performed; none is claimed here. If prior judgments are unavailable, narrow the study to retrospective narratives or visible decision processes. Missing reuse or payment data are not zeros.

## Six training components

| Component | Required action | Inspectable output |
|---|---|---|
| Customer identification | Specify who encounters the task, existing alternatives and the payer | Customer–task–alternative–payer map |
| Value explanation | Explain how value arises and supply a competing explanation | Falsifiable value hypothesis |
| Test design | Select evidence that separates explanations and could change action | Forecast, sample source and observation window |
| Failure diagnosis | Distinguish demand, adoption, implementation, business model and insufficient evidence | Weakened and surviving explanations |
| Resource allocation | Compare further testing, pivoting and stopping | Conditional decision and review point |
| Transfer | Compare old and new conditions and predict in a fresh project | New prediction and subsequent revision |

Duration and module weights require pilot work. The comparison activity should match instructional time, attention, task difficulty and access to evidence.

## Illustrative training cases

Case A is the merchant marketing tool in the main text. Users like the output but use it little. Participants choose evidence that distinguishes infrequent tasks, setup barriers and adequate free alternatives before recommending features or termination.

Case B is an inspection tool for property-management staff. Its surface features differ, yet it may share a separation between frontline users and a central payer. Participants identify which relationships might transfer and which conditions still need investigation.

Case C is another marketing tool, serving staff who frequently coordinate several stores. Similar appearance does not establish similar task frequency, alternatives or purchasing arrangements. Rejecting C because A failed could indicate negative transfer.

These are illustrative scenarios with no recruitment, transaction or retention results. Formal tasks require independently established evidence, benchmark outcomes and difficulty checks.

## A minimal behavioural design

A 2×2 design could cross AI access with structured training, estimating training effects, access effects and their interaction in new projects. Interactions may require larger samples than main effects. Preregistration should distinguish primary and secondary estimates. Team studies should randomize teams and account for repeated observations and clustering.

The sequence is baseline judgment, project A, training or a matched activity, fresh project B and delayed assessment. B includes both preserved relationships and changed decisive conditions. Separate independent performance from AI-assisted performance and balance test order to reduce answer leakage. Record model versions, prompts, memory, permissions, time and cost.

A separate study could compare customer-contact-first, prototype-first and continuing co-creation sequences. Define uncertainty types in advance and match overall resources and evidence opportunities. More customer-contact time should not be misinterpreted as an intrinsic sequence advantage. Combining every factor in the initial experiment would make interpretation and adequate power harder.

## Evaluating improvement

A candidate primary outcome is probability-forecast performance for specified customer behaviours in new projects. Define the customer population, event and observation window before forecasting. The Brier score is the mean of (p−y)², where p is the forecast probability and y indicates whether the event occurred. A lower score indicates better overall probability predictions. It is not a pure calibration measure and should be considered alongside calibration, discrimination and simple forecast benchmarks. Increased confidence alone does not establish learning.

Controlled tasks with explicit states, options and payoffs can measure mistaken continuation, mistaken termination and decision loss relative to a prespecified benchmark. Define loss weights in advance and report sensitivity to alternatives. In actual ventures, the commercial counterfactual following termination is usually unobserved; an error rate cannot be inferred directly without independent evidence or narrower claims.

Evidence choices, belief revision, reasons and subsequent customer behaviour provide complementary outcomes. Open-ended reasoning should be assessed blind to treatment where feasible. The model offering advice should not be the sole evaluator. Documentation length and retrospective writing quality do not substitute for transfer.

Power analysis should consider the smallest meaningful effect, primary comparisons, clustering, repeated cases and attrition. No sample size or achieved power is asserted here. Report intervals, missing-data rules, multiplicity decisions and the complete flow of observations, including null and adverse findings.

## Decision-record template

1. Project, date, customer and task.
2. Current decision and available actions.
3. Value hypothesis and competing explanations.
4. Prior probability forecast, event and observation window.
5. Recruitment source, non-participation and evidence gaps.
6. Results that would change the decision.
7. Actual observation, provenance and credibility.
8. Weakened and surviving explanations.
9. Reasons and costs for continuation, pivoting or termination.
10. Conditions under which the inference may hold and contexts where it should not generalize.
11. Conditions to check and outcomes to predict in the next project.
12. Revision when new evidence arrives.
