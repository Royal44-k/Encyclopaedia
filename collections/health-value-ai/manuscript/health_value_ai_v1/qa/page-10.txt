This Perspective is based on purposive reading across economic theory, decision support and 255 
selected health and AI studies. It is not a systematic review, and the examples do not estimate a 
common effect. Their populations, interventions and horizons differ. The proposed measures 
have not been validated together, preferences may change, and longer-term predictions can be 
weak even when short-term causal evidence is strong. Restricting the record to what can be 
measured may itself omit valued aspects of life; expanding it indefinitely can make it unusable. 260 
Both problems need participatory development and empirical assessment. 
Health economic evaluation in the AI era should make the relation between technical gains and 
lived consequences explicit. The proposed framework standardizes the questions, comparisons 
and evidence requirements while leaving room for different defensible choices. Its contribution 
will depend on whether it improves understanding and makes constraints visible at an acceptable 265 
burden. Productivity, enjoyment and health can each matter; the scientific task is to establish 
how they change under feasible alternatives, and the evaluative task is to explain how those 
changes bear on the lives people seek to lead. 
Data and code availability 
No new participant-level data were collected or analysed for this Perspective. The numerical 270 
findings cited in the text and Table 2 are reported by the original studies. The arithmetic example 
is hypothetical. The accompanying local evidence record and arithmetic script document this 
draft; a public repository has not been established.
