Health economic value in the AI era: a
framework for intertemporal choice
Richard T. Millian
Hangzhou Normal University
Higher income, faster task completion and convenience are readily visible economic gains. Changes in health,
recovery needs and time use are less consistently recorded. Artificial intelligence sharpens this problem: faster work
need not create more rest, and better assisted performance need not improve independent ability. Drawing on health
capital, time allocation, health capability and decision analysis, we propose a comparative health-consequence
framework. It compares feasible alternatives over a common horizon across resources and time, health and
functioning, experience, and capabilities and agency. Measurement, prediction and appraisal remain distinct. Selected
experiments and field studies show why outcomes with different units, timing and evidential scope should remain
visible rather than be reduced to compensation or a composite score. Applications to overtime, diet, sleep and AI use
illustrate the comparisons required. We develop testable propositions about the distribution of time savings,
persistence of benefits and decision quality, connecting health economic evaluation with everyday choice. The
framework requires prospective evaluation against established decision support.

From economic returns to changes in daily
life
An overtime payment and a faster AI-assisted task both offer
measurable gains. Whether either improves a person's life
depends on what changes with the gain: sleep opportunity,
workload, symptoms, relationships, disposable income and
the ability to pursue valued activities. The analytical
problem is therefore the translation from an observable
return into consequences experienced over time. A
comparison that records only the payment or task output
leaves this translation unexplained. Figure 1 locates the
problem across everyday settings; Figure 2 separates the
operations needed to address it.
The starting point is established economics. Grossman's
health-capital approach treats health as a stock relevant to
both well-being and productive time; Becker's
allocation-of-time framework makes non-market time part of
economic choice.[1, 2] Marx's discussion of the working day
directs attention to bargaining, limits on labour and the
reproduction of labour power.[3] These traditions motivate
different questions rather than a single interchangeable
theory. In particular, health losses cannot simply be
appended to Marxian surplus value as an empirically
measured quantity without a separate account of exposure,
counterfactual outcomes and incidence.
Health capability approaches further distinguish achieved
states from the real opportunities people have to maintain
health and act on their goals.[4] This matters when an
apparent choice cannot be refused without losing housing or
essential care. Evidence on health-dependent utility also
cautions against assuming that a given monetary increment
has the same welfare significance in every health state.[5]
Neither point makes income irrelevant. Resources can
enable care, security and participation; the question is which
consequences a particular increment enables or displaces
under actual constraints.
We use health economic value to mean the decision-relevant
significance of health and functioning within these
trade-offs. It is not synonymous with total welfare, earning
capacity or a person's worth. Money, time, experience and
agency enter the comparison because they interact with
health and carry value of their own. The proposed
contribution is an integrative accounting procedure: specify
a feasible counterfactual, trace consequences over time,
attach evidence and uncertainty, and make the evaluative
judgement explicit. A legitimate result can be a preference, a
conditional choice or an unresolved comparison.

From measurement to appraisal
Measurement describes a state; prediction estimates what
would happen under alternatives; appraisal asks how the
difference matters. A blood test belongs to the first
operation. Estimating how a dietary change affects a future
outcome belongs to the second. Deciding whether that
expected change justifies cost, effort or lost enjoyment
belongs to the third. Collapsing these operations can turn a
reference interval into a moral ranking, an association into a
personal forecast, or a stated preference into proof of
informed consent.
Monetary valuation can answer defined policy questions, but
compensation is not biological restoration. Conversely,
irreversibility does not logically make valuation impossible:
people still compare prevention, mitigation and foregone
opportunities. What fails is the inference that a payment
necessarily neutralizes a loss. A stated minimum payment
depends on income, alternatives and understanding;
accepting dangerous work under severe deprivation does not
establish that the health loss is trivial. Behavioural welfare
analysis provides reasons to examine the conditions under
which choices reveal evaluative preferences.[10]
The framework adapts the transparency of cost–consequence
analysis and the explicit criteria of multicriteria decision
analysis, while retaining their limitations.[8, 11] It adds no
validated universal scale. An established decision guide
already elicits options, uncertainty, values and support
needs.[12] Our narrower proposal makes time reallocation,
outcome trajectories, assisted versus unaided functioning
and the distribution of consequences explicit within such
support. Any advantage over existing tools must be
demonstrated, not inferred from a new label.
Standardized health-status instruments can supply
comparable information; preference-based measures such as
EQ-5D can support health-economic evaluation when used
with the appropriate valuation framework.[13] They do not
exhaust autonomy, relationships or personally valued
capability. Self-determination theory offers a
complementary account of autonomy, competence and
relatedness, without implying that every activity has the
same meaning for every person.[14] Reading, gaming,
exercise and companionship therefore enter as alternatives
whose consequences require assessment, rather than
predetermined categories of virtue or harm.
Table 1 provides a minimum record. Its purpose is
comparability of the decision process, not uniformity of
preferences. When meaningful consequences diverge, the
analyst should show the conflict and examine sensitivity to
priorities. A single index is optional only when its scales,
weights, uncertainty and double-counting risks are
defensible; declining to aggregate is often the more
informative result.

Intertemporal comparison and time
constraints
Let xi(a;τ) denote person i's consequence profile under
feasible option a at time τ. Components retain their own
units: disposable resources, time allocation, health and
functioning, experience and capabilities. For components
with a meaningful numerical difference, define:
 Δxi(a,b;τ) = xi(a;τ) − xi(b;τ). (1)
This is a contrast, not a causal prediction model. Categorical
changes remain descriptive. Every estimated component
requires a population, comparator, horizon, source and
uncertainty statement. Timing may require a trajectory rather
than an endpoint. Let zj denote an appropriately oriented
criterion, with larger values preferred within its applicable
range. Conditional dominance requires:
 Δzj ≥ 0 (∀j); Δzk > 0 (∃k). (2)
This ordering applies only if the criteria cover the relevant
consequences and uncertainty does not overturn it. Many
biomarkers are not monotonic, and omitted consequences
can reverse an apparent ranking. Where trade-offs remain,
personal appraisal is necessary; no universal exchange rate
follows from equations (1)–(2).
For AI-assisted work, let S = T0 − T1 be time saved on a
fixed task; T1 includes prompting, verification and rework.
Let A be time added through additional tasks and B all other
net changes in work time, defined to avoid overlap. Then:
 ΔW = −S + A + B. (3)
Figure 3 illustrates the identity. Saving four task hours
produces four fewer work hours if A = B = 0, one fewer if A
= 3 and B = 0, and one more if A = 5 and B = 0. These are
hypothetical allocations, not estimated AI effects. Even a fall
in total work does not determine health: the activities
replacing work, discretion over them and their subsequent
consequences remain to be measured.

Three levels of AI benefit
AI can expand productive capacity. In a randomized study of
453 professionals, Noy and Zhang found improvements in
writing-task speed and quality.[15] In a field study covering
5,172 customer-support agents, Brynjolfsson and colleagues
used staggered deployment and difference-in-differences
analysis; their preferred estimate implied about a 15%
increase in issues resolved per hour.[16] This was not a
randomized trial, and the field outcome was throughput, not
health. Together these studies support productivity gains
while leaving open who receives the time or income
dividend.
The relevant distinction is also between performance with
assistance and capability retained without it. Bastani and
colleagues' educational experiment illustrates why an
evaluation should include an unaided assessment rather than
infer learning from assisted practice alone.[19] This does not
establish that AI generally erodes cognition. It specifies a
measurement requirement: immediate performance, transfer
to unfamiliar tasks and independent performance answer
different questions. Accessibility and supported functioning
can be valuable even where unaided performance is
unchanged.
AI companionship raises a temporal distinction. In the
seven-day third study reported by De Freitas and colleagues,
loneliness relief was most clearly immediate; a persistent
improvement in pre-interaction loneliness was not
demonstrated.[17] Differential attrition and a
non-time-matched control limit interpretation. This is
evidence for examining when benefit occurs, not grounds for
dismissing companionship or diagnosing dependence. For
short-form content and virtual interaction, useful records
would include sleep displacement, enjoyment, relationships
and the ability to disengage; content type or duration alone
does not establish harm.
Information accuracy and user benefit also differ. Bean and
colleagues found that people working through hypothetical
medical scenarios did not reproduce the models' standalone
performance; Figure 5d retains the reported
control-versus-AI contrast.[18] The outcome was scenario
interpretation, not clinical disease. Earlier
Facebook-deactivation research likewise shows the value of
measuring consequences beyond platform use, although
social-media findings cannot simply be transferred to
generative AI.[20] Health-literate AI already emphasizes
comprehension, agency, accountability and
proportionality.[21] Our proposal complements that agenda
by specifying the consequences and time horizons to
compare, rather than claiming these concerns as new.

Choosing measures for the decision
Measurement starts with the decision. Comparing a meal, a
month of night work and an AI companion requires different
information. Feasible alternatives and a common
observation horizon should be established before choosing a
small set of relevant measures. Figure 4 organizes candidate
measures by their role in the comparison.
Separate exposures, states and consequences. Sleep
opportunity, sitting time, diet and screen use describe
behaviours or environments. Symptoms, laboratory
measures and task performance describe selected states.
Their significance depends on daily functioning, experience
and valued activities. The ICF helps situate functioning in
context.[6] Adding sedentary time, a correlated biomarker
and a downstream disease risk into a single score can count
the same pathway repeatedly. Candidate causal links should
therefore be specified before aggregation or mediation
analysis.
Clinical measures need clinical meaning. Glucose and lipid
measures, blood pressure, muscle function, vision or skin
symptoms may be relevant to a defined question; there is no
coherent omnibus measure of blood quality or bodily
superiority. Reference ranges depend on the measure and
context. Appearance alone cannot establish internal health,
and a change in appearance need not constitute disease.
Physical and psychological outcomes may influence one
another, but their association is not invariably positive or
causal. Disability, body size or a mental-health diagnosis
does not reduce a person's worth.
Experience and functioning should be observed at
appropriate times. WHO-5 records mental well-being over
the preceding two weeks; it is not a diagnostic test or an
instantaneous response measure.[7] A brief attention task, an
activity diary and a symptom report likewise capture
different constructs. Selection should consider reliability,
responsiveness, language, accessibility and burden.
Repeated measurement may distinguish transient discomfort
from a sustained trajectory, but collecting more data is not
automatically better and can increase anxiety or exclusion.
Finally, attach uncertainty to each consequential claim.
Distinguish observed findings from projections and
unknowns; an unmeasured effect is not zero. Report absolute
risks when defensible baseline estimates and applicable
models exist, rather than translating relative effects into
unsupported personal forecasts. Preserve the person's
priorities alongside the evidence, and revisit the comparison
if circumstances or outcomes change.

Empirical estimates and their scope
Figure 5 preserves four different estimands. Long working
hours were associated with stroke incidence in a cohort
meta-analysis; this does not estimate the effect of one
overtime week.[22] Hall and colleagues' 20-person inpatient
crossover experiment found greater energy intake on the
ultra-processed diet, whereas Tasali and colleagues'
sleep-extension trial found lower estimated energy intake
relative to control.[23, 24] The former interval is one
standard error, not a 95% confidence interval. Bean and
colleagues' odds ratio concerns identification in medical
scenarios, not the probability of illness.[18]
These findings make particular consequences credible
enough to investigate. They do not validate the entire
framework, establish a universal lifestyle hierarchy or
provide coefficients for pricing an individual's health.
Transporting an effect requires attention to population,
exposure, comparator, outcome and follow-up. The figure
therefore keeps study designs, units and uncertainty visible
instead of pooling unlike outcomes.

Trade-offs in practice
Consider a hypothetical 28-day window. Option A offers a
net supplement of CNY 10,000 for seven overtime days;
option B offers CNY 100,000 for 28. Holding ordinary
earnings equal, ΔY = 100,000 − 10,000 = 90,000 CNY (4),
alongside 21 additional overtime days. Figure 6 aligns the
denominators. It does not assign the remaining days in
option A a health value automatically. Their activities,
recovery opportunities and effects on family members must
be recorded; effects extending beyond day 28 require a
longer outcome horizon.
A disciplined appraisal asks about shift duration and timing,
baseline symptoms, the ability to stop, essential financial
needs and the next-best feasible option. The same schedule
may be evaluated differently under different constraints
without making its health consequences unreal. The
framework cannot determine that CNY 100,000 is sufficient
compensation. It can identify what is known, what remains
uncertain and which conflicts drive the decision. A legally or
contractually specified payment is an allocation of
responsibility; it is not evidence that lost functioning has
been restored.
Sleep restriction also shows why subjective adaptation and
objective function should be distinguished. In Van Dongen
and colleagues' laboratory programme, 35 adults were
randomized to 4, 6 or 8 hours in bed for 14 nights; a separate
total-deprivation experiment involved 13 adults.
Performance deficits accumulated in the 4- and 6-hour
groups, while reported sleepiness did not track their full
progression.[25] These functional findings do not establish
permanent neuronal destruction after one late night.
Recovery and persistence require their own evidence.
For food choices, the comparator includes price, preparation,
access, enjoyment and relevant health outcomes. A
controlled feeding effect cannot by itself show that a feasible
household substitution has the same effect or cost. For
leisure and AI companionship, compare what the activity
replaces and whether benefit persists, rather than presume
outdoor activity always dominates gaming or virtual contact.
The same procedure can recognize enjoyment and support
while detecting displaced sleep, unwanted use or burden. It
supports deliberation without prescribing a single ideal life.

Testable propositions and evaluation
Validation involves two questions: which mechanisms
change health-related outcomes, and whether the framework
improves informed choice. The first provides evidence for
its content; the second determines its practical value. Figure
7 separates these research questions, and Table 3 specifies
three propositions and observations that could weaken them.
An AI workplace study could combine task logs with
whole-day time diaries and repeated outcomes, while
measuring authority over workload and schedules. A
randomized organizational policy or a credible natural
experiment would strengthen causal inference about who
captures saved time. A simple association between AI use
and well-being would remain vulnerable to job selection,
workload, prior health and reverse causation. Mechanism
analysis should be prespecified; controlling mechanically for
post-treatment time use can obscure the total effect it seeks
to explain.
For the decision tool, an active comparator is essential. Both
groups should receive the same factual evidence and an
established guide; the intervention adds the explicit time,
consequence and uncertainty module (Fig. 7).
Decision-quality research and the Cochrane review of
patient decision aids inform the comparator and outcome
selection.[9, 26] The primary outcome should be specified
before recruitment, with independent scoring of
understanding rather than agreement with the author's
preferred lifestyle. Transfer to an unfamiliar decision,
values–choice concordance and calibrated uncertainty can be
secondary outcomes.
Evaluation must also capture completion time, attrition,
anxiety, accessibility and the experience of people who stop
using the tool. Repeated use may introduce learning, fatigue
or changes in the decision itself. A preregistered protocol
should specify the target population, effect of interest,
analysis of missingness, multiplicity and sample-size
justification. Interviews can explain comprehension or
disagreement but cannot replace causal estimates. If the
added module offers no meaningful benefit over the guide,
or its burden outweighs benefit, it should be simplified or
abandoned; a shift toward supposedly healthier choices
alone would not demonstrate success.

Institutions, distribution and research
priorities
A consequence profile would be incomplete if it treated
constrained behaviour as a failure of individual discipline.
Workplace rules, platform design, housing, food access and
caring responsibilities shape both available options and the
capacity to act on a preference. Figure 8 places these
conditions upstream of appraisal. Recording that someone
would prefer rest is insufficient if income insecurity or
workload rules prevent it. Decision support can reveal such a
conflict; it cannot itself redistribute bargaining power or
provide the missing option.
The perspective of the account must be stated. A worker's
net payment, an employer's productivity, a family's unpaid
care and public expenditure are distinct entries. A payment
can compensate a recipient while representing a transfer
between parties rather than a net social resource gain. Time
saved for one person may be shifted to another through
monitoring, correction or care. Comparing individual,
household and societal accounts can expose these
differences without assuming that their interests can be
aggregated by a single unexamined weight.
Institutions can also use the framework to ask concrete
questions: does a productivity policy return time to workers;
does a platform permit meaningful disengagement; and does
an intervention remain feasible for people with limited
money, mobility or digital access? These are evaluative
questions, not established effects of particular policies. The
same information should not become a surveillance score
used to penalize workers, deny opportunities or blame
people for constrained choices. Purpose limitation and
proportionate collection are part of making the proposed
support usable.
The central claim is modest in form but consequential in
practice: a gain is not fully characterized until the
health-relevant consequences, their timing and their
distribution are visible. AI strengthens the need for this
accounting because task performance, independent
capability and lived benefit can move differently. A common
comparison procedure can make these divergences
discussable without imposing a common preference. Its
scientific value will depend on whether it improves
explanation, prediction or decision quality beyond the
methods on which it builds.
This conceptual Perspective integrates economic theory,
measurement approaches and selected empirical studies
through narrative synthesis. The framework remains to be
validated. Its breadth may increase burden, population
effects may not transfer to individuals, and preferences may
change with circumstances. The next step is context-specific
feasibility research followed by comparison with established
decision support, assessing whether any gains in
understanding and choice persist.
