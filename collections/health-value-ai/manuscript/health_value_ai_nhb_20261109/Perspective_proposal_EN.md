# Perspective proposal

To: Editors, Nature Human Behaviour

## Proposed title

Health economic value and choice in the age of artificial intelligence

## Author

Richard T. Millian
Hangzhou Normal University

## Abstract

Overtime pay, convenient meals and faster work with artificial intelligence (AI) offer visible gains in money or time. Changes in sleep, physical burden and recovery may receive less attention. Drawing on health capital, time allocation, health capability and decision analysis, this Perspective proposes a framework for comparing health-related consequences. It compares feasible options over a common horizon, recording resources and time, health and functioning, experience, and capabilities and agency before appraisal against personal priorities. Measurement, prediction and judgement remain distinct; outcomes retain their units, timing and uncertainty. Faster tasks need not create more rest, and better assisted performance need not improve independent ability. Cases involving overtime, food choices and digital leisure illustrate the comparison. Testable propositions concern time allocation, persistence of effects and decision quality. The framework clarifies choices and consequences while allowing priorities to differ across people and circumstances.

## Relevance to a broad readership

Artificial intelligence is changing the relationship between task performance, the allocation of time and daily life. Productivity gains are readily measured, but they do not alone reveal whether people gain rest, preserve independent functioning or obtain lasting support. This Perspective connects these distinctions to health economics and behavioural decision research. Its central proposal is a structured comparison of feasible alternatives, with explicit timing, uncertainty and personal priorities. It does not assign a universal monetary price to health or prescribe a preferred lifestyle.

The article brings together health capital, time allocation, health capability, behavioural welfare and existing decision-support approaches. Four everyday cases show how the comparison can be applied to overtime, food and digital leisure. Selected published estimates illustrate what can be measured while preserving differences between outcomes and study designs. A research agenda separates testing the causal mechanisms from testing whether the proposed decision module adds value beyond established tools.

The proposed contribution is a conceptual synthesis and testable research agenda, not a validated scale or a report of new participant data. The main manuscript contains approximately 3,200 words and four figures. Supporting materials include three tables and four extended-data figures. I would welcome the editors’ assessment of the proposal’s suitability for a Perspective.
